Labor Efficiency Ratio, Explained

What LER actually measures, why it's the core metric in my practice, and how to move it without burning your team out.

Labor Efficiency Ratio — LER — is the relationship between what you pay your people and the revenue it services. Dollars out versus dollars in. It's the tenth step in the philosophy I use with every client, and it's the metric I keep coming back to because it's the one number that tells you, honestly, whether your team's time is effective.

What LER actually measures

Most agencies already track utilization: the percentage of a person's time that's billable. LER asks a blunter question: for every dollar you pay someone to produce client work, how many dollars does that work generate? Utilization tells you whether people are busy. LER tells you whether your effort is effective. An agency can run hot on utilization (everyone slammed, every hour logged against a job) and still be bleeding margin, because busy and effective aren't the same thing.

There is no "healthy" LER number. The right number for your firm depends on your service mix, your pricing, your cost of goods sold, and your salary cap. Changing those variables makes a single LER for all ineffective, but generally a 3.0 does good things for you.

At North Street Creative, my approach increased Labor Efficiency by 122% alongside a 25% increase in revenue and a 40% increase in profit. Even in a dire financial situation, with an exhausted line of credit, an overstaffed team, and erratic cash flow, we turned the tide by concentrating on simple measures, consistency, and by operating a learning organization.

If you measure only one thing, measure LER.

What it looks like in practice

Think of your P&L:

  1. Revenue
  2. Cost of goods sold
  3. Gross profit 👈 this is the real size of your firm
  4. Operating expenses (OPEX)
  5. Net profit

By moving all direct labor costs (labor you manage) to your OPEX you can isolate your gross profit and divide it by your direct labor:

$876,239 gross profit / $606,434 in direct labor = 1.44

3.0 means you pay $1 in direct labor for every $3 of gross profit it produces 1.44 means you pay $1 in direct labor for every $1.44 of gross profit it produces

This is not a hard number to calculate, but it tells you about the fortunes of the company, which is close to ruin at a 1.44.

At 1.44, only about $270K is left to cover management salaries, overhead, and profit. It's not likely to end well. As Hemingway put:

“How did you go bankrupt?” Bill asked. “Two ways,” Mike said. “Gradually and then suddenly.”

How to move it without burning the team out

Change what your team's hours are spent on, not by demanding more hours. Reallocate who does the work. Giving people the authority to decide without waiting on the owner. Build simple, consistent, repeating patterns that improve communication and trust.

When owners hear "improve labor efficiency" it's easy to overreact with aggression: work more hours, cut headcount, push harder. That's how you improve the ratio for a quarter and wreck the agency for a year. None of my results came from working the team harder. They came from redirecting what the team was working on.

Some things we can improve immediately, others take years

The first lever is the Talent Siphon — building a real labor advantage over your competitors without overpaying, which mostly means not asking senior people to do junior work and not asking juniors to do senior work unsupervised. Misallocated labor is invisible on a utilization report and glaring on an LER report, because it's expensive hours producing cheap outcomes.

The second is Command by Intent: giving your people the context to make decisions without you in the room. Every decision that has to wait for the owner is an hour someone else is paid for but can't spend producing. That's dollars out with nothing coming in yet.

The third is treating junior talent as your future rather than your bottleneck — pairing seniors who teach with juniors who are given real, appropriately-scoped work, so the agency's capacity grows without every hour costing senior-level dollars. And underneath all three is process: I install the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS) as standard, because a documented process is what lets efficiency gains survive contact with a bad week, instead of evaporating the moment the owner looks away.

That's the difference between improving LER and burning out a team to fake it for a quarter: the first one is systems thinking — building the process so your people are doing the parts only humans can do well, instead of running them harder inside a broken one.
