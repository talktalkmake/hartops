Your agency's numbers are always a month late. That's the leak.

Most owners run on a P&L that arrives three weeks after the month it describes. The fix for margin isn't another report. It's timeliness.

Every agency I work with has a month-end ritual. The books close, someone reconciles, and a week or two later a profit-and-loss statement lands in the owner's inbox. They open it, wince or smile, and file it away. The number is real. It is also useless — not because it's wrong, but because it's late.

By the time you read May's P&L, May is gone. The over-serviced client has already been over-serviced. The project that quietly slid from profitable to underwater did so three weeks ago, and you paid for every one of those hours. The report isn't a steering wheel. It's a rear-view mirror, and a foggy one.

Late numbers don't inform decisions. They grade them.

The thing that actually moves agency margin is the speed of the loop between work happening and you seeing it. Margin in a service business is mostly the labor efficiency ratio — the gross profit you earn for every dollar of delivery payroll. It's a number you can wreck in a single week by putting the wrong people on the wrong work, or by letting a fixed-fee project run 40% long without anyone flagging it.

You can't manage that ratio from a monthly report. The damage compounds daily and the report arrives monthly. So owners do the only thing the data lets them do: they react after the fact. They have the "what happened on Brand X" conversation in week six, when the honest version of that conversation was available — if anyone could have seen it — in week one.

More dashboards won't fix it. They usually make it worse.

The instinct, when the numbers feel out of reach, is to buy a more elaborate reporting tool. Another dashboard. More charts. But the bottleneck was never the chart. It's the input. The data is late because capturing it is friction, and friction means time entry happens on Friday afternoon (or the following Tuesday) as a guessing exercise, if it happens at all. A beautiful dashboard built on time logged from memory three days later is a confident-looking fiction.

So the real lever is upstream of any report: make capturing the work so fast and so close to the moment it happens that the data is simply there, current, every day. Get that right and the dashboards become almost incidental. The numbers are trustworthy because they're fresh, and they're fresh because logging them cost nobody anything.

What changes when the numbers show up the same day the work does

  • The over-servicing conversation happens in week one, while it's still cheap to fix.
  • You see which projects are bleeding margin before the month closes, not after.
  • Capacity stops being a gut feel. You can say yes to the next piece of work because you can see the room for it.
  • Your team spends seconds logging time instead of dreading a weekly reconstruction — so the data you steer by is actually real.
