David Maister said the following more than 30 years ago and, as in most matters, he's both right and more right than ever: 

> The assets employed in the business are ... Staff, whose salaries are comparable to assets financed by debt, at a fixed interest rate.

### What does that mean? 

First let's set aside the ugly connotation that people can be reduced to numbers, to 'things.' We're talking about people being assets because they change over time, for better or worse, for both themselves and their employer. It means the people in the company need to deliver on the investment the company makes in them. And it means — sorry, owner-operators — that it's management's responsibility to improve the value of the asset over time. Put differently, it is the empoloyees responsibility to add value in the short-term; it's the company's responsibility to add value in the long-term.

### This is going to feel uncomfortable

It's natural to laud high performers. We want people to succeed and be happy in their job. But, as every former piano player knows, without practice your skill only declines. By asking the profitable (P&L) people to repeat their days we neglect the balance sheet of their career. As an owner-operator you know that by focusing only on the here and now you're reducing your ability to react to market forces. Why would it be any different for your employees? 

### 'We have high standards'

Be honest. When was the last time you fired someone who went from vital to painful? It's common for me to see owners head-in-hands when talking about _that_ employee. It's uncommon to see owners fire people after fair warning and a reasonable path to save themselves. The irony? Employees want high standards. They want to do good work. They want to learn constantly. And they can smell blood (and BS) when it comes to 'company culture.' Your best defense against employees who care too little is to care more about their career than _this_ job you're employing them to do. Separate pride in your team from high standards, because you might be mistaking the former for the latter. Instead: 

- Give them access to data. 
- Give them simple golden rules. 
- Give them decisions to make. 

Micromanagement is commonly understood (you probably had an overbearing, stifling manager in a previous job). A distrustful, anxious manager can be very different, less obvious, and more (common-sense) defensible. 

You've let people go in the past. You've avoided letting people go whose absence would cause short-term discomfort. If your people wonder _what does it take to get fired around here?_ you know you don't have high standards. You can have good culture, good people, quality work, a good reputation, happy employees and low professional standards. Nothing predicts performance better than professional standards, and while they're not mutually exclusive, poor performance and poor standards are often found together. 

## More balance sheet, less P&L

I'm no Einstein, but the guy who said the following was: 

> The most powerful force in the Universe is compound interest

If you want better students you need better teachers. And you need to be prideful that your company is a learning company, because without continuously developing your teaching muscle, it will atrophy. If a company is truly _seniors only_, what does that say about their ability to teach? To price competitively? To think out loud through quality content? A company of only seniors: 

- Has no leverage: every billable hour struggles to be profitable. 
- Is full of tenured, smart professionals who've been there and seen it all; they struggle to put in the legwork professional services inevitably requires (they're not going to work all weekend when you need them to). 
- Has no time to dwell on what comes next, nor time to form new ideas that strengthen the positioning of the company (they're too busy working every billable hour possible). 

By balancing a team with both junior and senior professionals you invite mistakes. By designing systems which provide a safety zone that prevents those mistakes being public you begin a constant dialog between juniors and seniors that begins the talent siphon in your business. Seniors trade insight, knowledge, and nous for effort with the juniors. The company begins to learn how to codify their inner workings, to simplify how tasks go from A to Z. You need to find people willing to learn (listen and they will tell you) for the learning company to take effect, but once it does it immediately begins returning dividends. Small at first, but _compounding over time_. 

The alternative is to struggle with people too smart and too experienced to put in the required effort. It may calm your nerves putting only your 'best' people on assignments they've done a thousand times before, but the opportunity cost will find you sooner or later.
