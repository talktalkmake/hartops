Your agency's numbers are always a month late

Why timeliness — not another report — is the real lever for agency margin.

Every agency I work with has a month-end ritual. The books close, someone reconciles, and a week or two later a profit-and-loss statement lands in the owner's inbox. They open it, wince or smile, and file it away. The number is real. It's also nearly useless — not because it's wrong, but because it's late.

By the time you read May's P&L, May is gone. The over-serviced client has already been over-serviced. The project that quietly slid from profitable to underwater did so three weeks ago, and you paid for every one of those hours. The report isn't a steering wheel. It's a rear-view mirror, and a foggy one.

Late numbers don't inform decisions. They grade them.

Margin in a service business is mostly a question of efficiency: the gross profit you earn for every dollar of delivery payroll. It's a number you can damage in a single week — by putting the wrong people on the wrong work, or by letting a fixed-fee project run 40% long with no one flagging it.

You can't manage something that moves daily with a report that arrives monthly. So owners do the only thing the data allows: they react after the fact. The honest "what happened on Brand X" conversation gets had in week six, when the version that could have changed the outcome was available — if anyone could have seen it — in week one.

More dashboards won't fix it

The instinct, when the numbers feel out of reach, is to buy a more elaborate reporting tool. Another dashboard, more charts. But the bottleneck was never the chart. It's the input. The data is late because capturing it is friction — so time entry happens on Friday afternoon as a guessing exercise, if it happens at all. A beautiful dashboard built on hours reconstructed from memory three days later is a confident-looking fiction.

The real lever sits upstream of any report: make capturing the work fast enough, and close enough to the moment it happens, that the data is simply there — current, every day. Get that right and the reports almost take care of themselves.

What changes when the numbers are current

  • The over-servicing conversation happens in week one, while it's still cheap to fix.
  • You see which projects are bleeding margin before the month closes, not after.
  • Capacity stops being a gut feel — you can say yes to new work because you can see the room for it.
  • The data you steer by is real, because logging it cost your team almost nothing.

If your numbers are always a month behind, you don't have a reporting problem. You have a timeliness problem, and it's quietly costing you margin every week you tolerate it. That's a fixable leak — and finding leaks like it is most of what a diagnostic is for.

(It's also the thinking behind VERA, the tool I now build — but the principle holds whatever you use to capture the work.)
